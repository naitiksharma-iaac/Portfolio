This project investigates how heterogeneous reclaimed timber can be treated as a measurable stock rather than a standardised catalogue. The workflow begins with irregular material already present on site and asks how it can be sorted, characterised, structurally allocated and assembled through local rules into an architectural form.

Emergence and Assemblage

Designing the system, not the single outcome

Traditional computational design often starts from a fixed geometry and then optimises it within a predefined envelope. This approach is powerful, but it tends to privilege the final object over the logic that produces it.

Assemblage-based design works differently. The designer defines parts, rules, fields, behaviours, and limits, and the architectural result emerges from local interactions between them. In this sense, the project is not a direct optimisation of one ideal form; it is a search for a generative system capable of accommodating material variation without losing structural intent.

Reclaimed Timber Problem

Non-standard stock and low reuse value

Reclaimed timber is difficult to reuse architecturally because it arrives in non-standard dimensions, irregular geometries, uncertain quality, and mixed conditions. Demolition stock includes defects, unsorted members, variable lengths, wear and local damage, all of which make conventional construction workflows uneasy.

Yet this heterogeneity also creates an opportunity. When stock is digitised, sorted, graded and allocated with awareness of available dimensions, the material can become an inventory rather than a liability. A circular construction logic emerges: heterogeneity is read as information, the stock is mapped and reused, and virgin-material dependency is reduced.

Research Question

How the project is framed

How can reclaimed timber stock be characterised, structurally allocated and aggregated through stress-informed fields into architectural assemblages?

Discrete Design as Intervention

From stock to assembly

The central intervention is a chain of transformations: irregular stock is discretised into a kit of parts, then composed through rule-based assembly into a structural architectural construction. Instead of insisting that each component be uniform, the method learns to work with difference by translating it into measurable variation and rule-compatible behaviour.

IRREGULAR STOCK → DISCRETISATION → KIT OF PARTS → RULE-BASED ASSEMBLY → ARCHITECTURAL CONSTRUCTION

Overall Workflow

Methodological sequence

Feature Extraction and Clustering

Characterising timber into usable part families

The project begins by scanning 55 timber elements from the available reclaimed stock. This matters because the material cannot be designed without first being measured: dimensions are uncertain, geometry varies, and quality is not known in advance. Digitisation turns a loose material stream into an inventory with readable characteristics.

Feature extraction then determines the timber’s measurable descriptors: oriented bounding box, longitudinal area, effective length, straightness, compactness and taper. These parameters create a comparable language for an otherwise heterogeneous stock and allow the project to move from physical variability to design logic.

The feature views below show how those descriptors are extracted from the scanned dataset. Effective length follows each member’s principal axis; the oriented bounding box records its aligned extents; longitudinal area captures the changing profile along that axis; and straightness measures deviation from a direct member trajectory. Together they convert visible irregularity into data that can be compared, scored and clustered.

Timber Stock Characterisation

Measuring variation before allocation

Characterisation turns the reclaimed stock into a measurable inventory. Across the scanned elements, length, volume and longitudinal area describe the available material, while straightness, taper and compactness capture geometric variation that is otherwise difficult to compare by eye.

These distributions support a transparent scoring process: the score and grade summarise the feature-based reading of each piece, while the validation and material-space views show how the descriptors relate across the stock. The correlation matrix makes those relationships explicit before pieces are grouped into proxy families and used by the aggregation rules.

Design Target

Bridge design versus truss design

The design target was a bridge-like spanning system rather than a fixed truss envelope. The project compares these two structural conditions to establish where the material system can remain expressive and where it must respond more rigidly to structural demand.

Structural Fields / TO

Topology optimisation setup and structural design domain

Topology optimisation is used to establish the structural design domain and guide the allocation of material across the bridge. The intention is not a disguised design form, but a field-based description of where material can be distributed to carry load efficiently.

Structural Fields

Translating structural demand into local information

The design target remains a bridge / truss-like structural condition. A field-based analysis translates that objective into spatial information: Von Mises stress magnitude identifies where the system experiences high demand, principal stress-flow directions define preferred orientations, and weighted regional zones help classify where rules should be more or less active.

These fields do not dictate a single final form; instead, they produce the conditions under which local aggregation can happen. The system becomes responsive to structural need while still preserving the irregularity and variability of the material stock.

Rule Classes

Three connection behaviours

The aggregation grammar is built from three principal rule classes: vertical stacking, horizontal stacking and inclined connection. Each produces a different spatial behaviour, and the structural field determines where each rule is activated.

Generated Options

Comparing outputs from the aggregation grammar

Different rule combinations produce different part systems and materially different timber-piece counts. The comparison keeps several generated outputs visible rather than presenting one predetermined form.

Field-Constrained Aggregation

Rules, regions and material respond together

The final aggregation is the outcome of a local interaction between parts, rules, regions and fields. The system does not simply place members in a geometric pattern; it responds to where structural demand is high, how the stock is distributed, and which connection types are permitted in each part of the field.

The final model shown here uses 678 parts and demonstrates the capacity of the method to transform irregular stock into a more coherent architectural structure. The result sits between raw material inventory and constructed assembly.

Field-constrained aggregation: local connection rules operate across the span to produce a larger structural network between two support zones.

Knowledge and Precedents

Structural force flow and material allocation

The methodology draws on two related lines of inquiry. The first is structural force flow, where the idea is that force paths and stress trajectories can inform how discrete components orient themselves in a larger system. This relates to work by Nervi and to segmented shell / slab research that treats structural information as an active design driver rather than a post-rationalisation check.

The second is material allocation: matching available timber inventory to the demands of the design. An allocation study based on a Hungarian algorithm was investigated as a methodological probe, but the final implementation remains a field-guided assembly process with a clear distinction between what was materially implemented and what was explored as a research method.

Structural Evaluation

The structural model reduces the aggregation to beam centre lines, beam contact faces and connector lines. This simplified representation allows the system to be evaluated in terms of buckling strength, utilisation and displacement, providing a performance lens without losing the discrete, material-driven logic that generated the form.

Current and Future Work

What is implemented and what remains open

The current project focuses on inventory mapping, feature scoring, clustering, field-driven aggregation, and structural evaluation as an integrated workflow. This is the implemented core of the research.

Future development extends the system toward material passports, a combinatorial construction kit, performative material allocation, AR assembly guidance and graph-based disassembly. These are important trajectories, but they are not presented as completed outcomes of the current project.

Reflection

The experiment demonstrates a way to move from irregular timber stock to a structurally informed assembly process. It keeps material uncertainty visible, uses local rules to coordinate large-scale behaviour, and frames reuse not as a generic sustainability principle but as an operational design methodology. The result is a research path toward circular construction built on material recovery, measurable stock and field-based assembly logic.
