Rhizome

Infrastructure as public space.

How can a space station grow into a place to live together? Rhizome proposes a modular settlement in low Earth orbit, expanding from six to 148 inhabitants through a network of shared spaces and distributed services.

Our three-person infrastructure group developed the growth strategy, nodes and connectors that link the wider studio’s living, working, greenhouse and recreation modules. Computational aggregation and collaborative BIM connected the scale of the station to the design of its individual parts.

01 / The proposition

An infrastructure for
long-term habitation.

The project brings together the systems needed to sustain a settlement and the spaces that make it habitable. Our infrastructure network distributes life support and energy while providing places to gather, move, store equipment and adapt the interior to daily activities.

The proposal studies a near-equatorial orbit approximately 400–500 km above Earth. Access, resupply, radiation and thermal conditions informed the design brief. Modules were conceived for prefabrication on Earth and assembly in orbit, with future reuse and robotic construction treated as longer-term possibilities.

02 / Rules for an expandable station

A common geometry.
Room for different programmes.

A truncated octahedron provides a space-filling geometric starting point. Its hexagonal and square faces allow connections in several directions. The infrastructure network uses nodes and connectors to establish a framework into which the other programme groups can introduce their own modules.

In Grasshopper, WASP describes which parts may connect and how an assembly can grow. Connection rules and constraints make the modular system explicit. The group explored aggregation strategies, including stochastic and field-based approaches, as well as single- and dual-ownership models.

The growth studies test how accommodation, food production, work and recreation can expand around the infrastructure. Each configuration keeps the relationship between the common network and the programme modules visible.

Selected stages from the group’s aggregation study. Colour distinguishes the programme families.

03 / Construction and services

A kit of parts that
carries the services.

The module system combines a primary frame with replaceable insulation panels, windows and hatches. Perforated structural ribs provide routes for wiring and pipework, while corner connections provide attachment points between components.

The proposed environmental control and life-support system (ECLSS) and building services run around the main structure. Distributing service capacity through the station allows new modules to connect as the settlement grows.

The energy proposal combines deployable circular photovoltaic arrays with permanent arrays. Their position is part of the growth strategy, preserving opportunities to extend the station. These are design proposals within the academic study, rather than tested spaceflight systems.

04 / Infrastructure as public space

Living within
the service network.

Nodes and connectors support activities as well as movement. The group explored interior arrangements for gathering, equipment, healthcare, communication and storage, giving the infrastructure a social role within the settlement.

Storage becomes part of the spatial design. Modular racks and packages can support seats, tables and personal spaces, while handles help inhabitants navigate and occupy the interior in microgravity. The render makes this relationship between body, equipment and enclosure visible.

05 / The computational workflow

Geometry, shared data
and building information.

The advanced part of the workflow is the connection between tools. A module is defined geometrically, combined through aggregation rules, exchanged between groups and developed into building information. The web configurator gives the station another form of exploration and communication.

This made the project a study in computational collaboration as well as space architecture: different teams could develop their own programme modules while working towards a common assembly.

06 / From aggregation to documentation

The model becomes
a documented assembly.

The integrative modelling submission develops the infrastructure as a BIM package, with cut and exploded axonometrics, plans, sections, elevations and component schedules. The drawings connect the spatial proposal to identifiable construction elements.

The smooth-cluster sheet separates exterior and interior panels, trims, structural angles and connectors. Its schedule records 144 exterior panels, 144 interior panels, 96 trims, 96 framing elements and 60 connectors for that particular assembly. This is where an abstract modular idea becomes countable and communicable.

07 / Group workflow and contribution

A shared system,
developed across teams.

Clemens Russ, Oluwadamilola (Dami) Akinniyi and I formed the infrastructure group within the wider orbit studio. We coordinated with the living, working, recreation and greenhouse groups, bringing their module geometries into the station’s shared framework.

Our workflow documentation distinguishes research, exploration, aggregation development and BIM production. During exploration, it identifies my work on connection and ownership rules, Clemens’ manual aggregation studies, and Dami’s part catalogue and node/connector modules. Later production diagrams record my work on renders, with drawing and presentation tasks shared across the team.

Speckle supported model exchange. Miro, Slack, Zoom and shared files supported discussion and coordination. The group’s aim was to keep information available and tasks transferable as the project developed.
