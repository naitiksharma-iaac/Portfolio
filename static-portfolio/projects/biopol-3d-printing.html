BioPol investigates how a design can adapt to the behaviour of a freshly extruded biopolymer composite. The team develops a segmented vault, isolates one arch for prototyping, and uses non-planar slicing and a supplied learning framework to explore the relationship between deposition, overhang and stability.

01 / Material behaviour

A fabrication process that must accommodate variation

BioPol explores robotic extrusion with biopolymer composites. The introductory lecture frames the material as a mixture of a natural binder, water, a plasticizer and fillers or fibres. Its example matrix combines xanthan gum with water and glycerol, alongside wood flour and possible fibre additions. This establishes the workshop’s material context rather than a fixed recipe for every printed specimen.

Freshly deposited material can spread, sag or compress under subsequent layers. A geometric model alone cannot fully describe those changes. The workshop therefore asks how fabrication decisions can respond to the material as an active part of the design process.

02 / Research question

Non-planar slicing as the central design challenge

What kinds of geometry are better suited to non-planar slicing than to planar slicing? The team develops this question through overhangs, surface texture and the orientation of printed layers.

Conventional horizontal slices create steps on inclined surfaces. Curved slices offer a way to follow a component’s geometry more closely. The presentation also proposes alignment with stress paths and improved layer contact as design motivations. These remain hypotheses to investigate through fabrication rather than measured strength improvements.

03 / Global geometry

A vault assembled from a series of arches

The architectural proposal is a vault formed by a sequence of segmented arches. Variations in span, height and subdivision create a family of related geometries instead of repeating one identical brick. The larger design study ranges from 16 to 31 segments across its arch series.

Form-finding informs the global geometry before the team isolates one arch for printing. This connects the overall structure to a manageable fabrication experiment: the vault establishes the design ambition, while individual components expose the practical limits of the process.

04 / Prototype arch

Twelve distinct brick segments

The team reduces the larger vault to a prototype arch composed of 12 brick segments. Each segment carries its own geometry and position in the assembly. Component dimensions and interfaces translate the continuous arch into pieces that can be printed separately.

The drawings make the relationship between the whole and its parts explicit. A component’s location determines its inclination, contact surfaces and overhang conditions, so printability becomes part of the subdivision strategy.

05 / Non-planar toolpaths

Curved layers, outer walls and infill

Non-planar slicing generates deposition paths that follow a changing surface rather than a stack of horizontal planes. The team studies how those surfaces pass through each brick and how the resulting paths connect the outer wall to the internal infill.

The infill provides local support to the perimeter as printing progresses. Its spacing and connection to the outer layers affect bridging distances and the unsupported portions of each bead. The toolpath must therefore resolve both the component’s final form and the sequence in which that form becomes stable.

06 / Deposition and stability

Print speed changes the bead

The workshop links material density and pressure to material flow, then relates that flow to print speed and bead width. At a given flow, changing travel speed changes how much material is deposited along a unit length of path.

A physical comparison uses normal and doubled print speed to examine the resulting deposition. Other trials focus on sagging in Brick 7 and overhang in Brick 1. These observations connect the abstract parameters to visible behaviour at the nozzle and in the printed wall.

07 / Learning workflow

Evaluating geometry with the supplied framework

The introductory lecture establishes two complementary approaches: offline pre-training in simulation and correction from experience during fabrication. The student task focuses on applying the provided reinforcement-learning framework to challenging geometries and exploring its evaluation functions.

An agent observes a state, chooses an action and receives a reward that influences its policy. In this workshop, the framework represents material effects such as sagging, bridging, compression and bead width. The team compares the supplied model with an alternative set of reward and penalty settings, including the balance between the surface and infill.

The design contribution lies in the geometry, toolpaths and evaluation choices, together with the interpretation of the printing trials.

08 / Model evaluation

Velocity, bead width and predicted deformation

The evaluation visualises several quantities along the toolpath: print velocity, bead width, displacement and an assumed collapse effect. Reading these together reveals where the model changes deposition and where the geometry remains difficult to support.

The workshop compares model configurations on Brick 8, then examines Brick 7. Their reported ranges belong to specific model runs, so they should not be read as universal limits or as proof that one configuration is always better. Predicted displacement and collapse are simulation outputs that require comparison with physical specimens.

09 / Printing and feedback

The specimen tests the prediction

The printed components reveal how closely the simulated behaviour describes the real material. Brick 8 shows substantial distortion and irregular deposition, while the Brick 7 specimen records the layered surface and internal opening produced by the toolpath.

These results make failure useful as evidence. Local sagging, loss of shape and uneven beads point back to the combination of geometry, support, flow and speed. The next iteration must refine those relationships rather than judge the workflow only by the appearance of the digital model.

10 / Outcome and reflection

A partial prototype and a fabrication method

The workshop presentation records five printed bricks out of the 12 planned segments. It documents a partial fabrication study rather than a completed printed vault. The architectural visualisation describes the intended assembly at a larger scale.

The project establishes a practical relationship between global geometry and local material behaviour. Arch subdivision determines the bricks, non-planar slicing determines their deposition paths, and model evaluation suggests adjustments that the physical print can then test.

The remaining work is to calibrate those predictions against measured specimens, improve unstable regions and complete the arch. The workshop’s central lesson is that designing with a variable material includes designing how the fabrication process learns from it.
